# Round 4: the final interview (co-founder + Head of GTM)

## What it tests

Values, judgement, motivation, and whether they'd trust you to own the GTM system alone. Reported for
another role: a culture-fit conversation with the CEO, behavioural questions. Possibly: "Tell us about
a time you hacked a (non-computer) system to your advantage."

## The story structure (60–90 seconds each)

**Situation** (one or two sentences) → **What you did** (most of the time; say "I", not "we") →
**Result** (a number if you have one) → **What you learned** or would do differently.

Every story must be true; they will ask follow-ups ("what exactly did you do?", "what would you change?").

## "Tell us about a time you hacked a (non-computer) system to your advantage"

**What they're testing:** do you notice how systems really work (rules, incentives, how people
decide), find a clever *fair* shortcut, and act on it? It's the "hacky" value, and the core instinct of
a GTM engineer.

**Structure:**

1. The system and how it normally works.
2. **What you noticed that others missed** (the most important part).
3. What you did.
4. The result, with a number.
5. One line connecting it to GTM.

**Where real stories hide:** competitions (did you shape your pitch around how judges actually score?
You've won several: ISTE Ideathon, Eco Fest pitch, ATOM Symposium, IIT BHU), college systems
(timetables, scarce slots, exam prep), getting replies from busy people, queues, bookings, paperwork,
job hunting.

**The shape of a good answer (an illustration, not your story):**

> "Judges see 40 pitches in a day and remember few. I noticed they score from a printed rubric, so I
> ordered my pitch exactly by the rubric and used its words as my section titles. It made scoring easy,
> and we placed first. GTM is the same: if you understand how the buyer decides, you make it easy for
> them to say yes."

Use your own true story; never cheating, never harming anyone.

## Questions they may ask, and what's behind them

| Question | What they want to hear |
| --- | --- |
| Why do you want to be the *first* GTM engineer? | You want ownership and ambiguity, not a ticket queue |
| What would you build first, and why? | Listen to reps first; quick win (inbound routing or dedupe); then the foundation (see [12-systems-thinking/03](../12-systems-thinking/03-prioritisation-and-first-90-days.md)) |
| How do you handle unclear priorities? | Talk to users, estimate impact vs effort, decide, communicate in writing |
| How do you work with salespeople? | Sit with them, watch their day, ship small wins, explain simply |
| How do you make sure what you build keeps working? | Logging, alerts, tests, idempotent re-runs, runbooks |
| How do you use AI responsibly here? | Rules vs AI, validation, allow-listed fields, human review, logging |
| Where do you see yourself in 2–3 years? | Growing the GTM system (and maybe a team) with them; owning more |
| What would make you leave? | Honest, not negative: e.g. not being able to ship or learn |
| What's something you've changed your mind about? | Shows learning and humility |

## Questions to ask the co-founder

- "What does the GTM team look like in a year, and how should the system grow with it?"
- "What's the biggest risk to your growth targets that better GTM systems could reduce?"
- "How do you see agentic workflows changing how the sales team works here?"
- "What did the best hires at Secfix do in their first three months?"

## End result

Five true, crisp stories and a hack answer that show ownership, judgement and the builder's instinct,
delivered in your own words.
